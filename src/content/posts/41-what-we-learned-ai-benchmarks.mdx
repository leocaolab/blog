We started this series with two goals: **high performance** — can sub-interpreters
carry a real multi-core web server? — and **pure AI** — could a pipeline build the
whole thing, Rust engine, unsafe C-API, benchmarks, and these posts, with no human
writing code? Both held up. And building it handed us a third goal we hadn't
started with. So we're ending with three.

## The first two held up

**Sub-interpreters delivered.** Multiplying the GIL — N interpreters, N cores,
full isolation — got a Python server to within a few percent of pure-Rust
throughput, survived long soak tests, and beat the multi-process Python field
by a wide margin. The model has real costs (data copies at the boundary, memory
per interpreter), but as a path to multi-core Python it's the real thing.

On raw request-handling we feel we've come close to the ceiling of what a Python
server can do — but *close* isn't *done*. There's still real headroom in the
places we haven't fully pushed: **async database access**, **JSON serialization**,
and **hardware-specific tuning**. The speed story isn't finished — it's just
paused behind a different one.

**And the AI built all of it.** That was the bet nobody had really made — point an
AI at hardcore systems optimization, not CRUD, and see how far it gets. It got
far.

## Why AI is *better* at this than at normal coding

The surprising part: performance work turned out to be a *better* fit for AI than
ordinary feature coding — because of the benchmark.

Most coding tasks have a fuzzy success signal. "Is this code good?" is a matter of
taste, and AI is happy to produce plausible-looking code that's subtly wrong.
Optimization doesn't work like that. A benchmark is a **continuous, objective,
un-bullshittable signal**. Change something, run wrk, read the number. The loop is
tight and the feedback is honest: measure → optimize → measure again. The AI can
argue its way past a code reviewer, but it cannot argue its way past 225 req/s.
That number is the truth every single iteration, and it dragged the whole project
forward.

## The flip side — and why the benchmark is a guardrail, not just a throttle

The honest counterexample is the most useful thing in this series. Early on, the
AI confidently concluded that free-threaded Python "lost" to sub-interpreters
because of atomic-refcount overhead — and wrote a whole crisp, mechanistic
explanation for it. It had copied that conclusion straight from an internal
benchmark doc. It sounded right. It was wrong — but note *exactly* what was wrong. Not that the
tax exists: the atomic-refcount overhead is real, and it's roughly the ~2–3% edge
sub-interpreters hold over free-threading in a fair fight. The error was calling
that a *decisive loss* on the strength of a "benchmark" that had never actually
exercised free-threading at all. A fresh, controlled experiment showed the two
approaches are throughput peers.

What caught it wasn't a smarter model. It was a human asking "are you sure?" and
then a *new benchmark*. That's the real lesson: without a measurement to check
against, an AI will state a plausible falsehood with total confidence. The
benchmark isn't just what pushes the AI forward — it's the guardrail that catches
its confident mistakes. No benchmark, no ground truth, no trust.

## The debugging genuinely surprised us

We expected code generation. We didn't expect investigation. Chasing the
cross-thread `PyThreadState` leak — a bug hidden behind three plausible red
herrings — the AI did delta debugging, **downloaded profiling tools, read flame
graphs**, wrote a standalone C reproducer to isolate the cause, and worked the
hypothesis space down to the real culprit. Mostly on its own, without a lot of
prompting. That's not autocomplete finishing a line; that's an investigator
narrowing a search.

## Goal 3, the one we added: high maintainability

We didn't start out testing this one — building the first two forced it onto the
list. Because the one weakness AI-built code reliably has is **quality**. Speed
accretes; so does mess. You can push the numbers higher on a codebase that's
quietly turning into a liability, and all you've built is a fast liability. So the
target grew: not just fast, not just AI-built, but *maintainable* — and that turns
out to be the hardest of the three.

So we ran [**arc**](https://github.com/leocaolab/getarc) — our own code-quality
tool — over the whole codebase. It surfaced **several hundred issues** and
**auto-fixed a portion of them**. The majority it deliberately left *unfixed for
now* — not because they don't matter, but because most deserve a real, systematic
fix rather than a quick patch. Rather than paper over them by hand, we're waiting
for **a better version of arc** to handle them properly. Optimizing harder on top
of unresolved quality debt is exactly the trap we're refusing to walk into.

So the next chapter isn't more throughput — it's cleanup, hardening, and
maintainability, AI-driven and arc-guided. And that's really the point: this
started as a two-goal experiment and grew a third. It could grow a fourth.

This isn't a finish line. It's the honest state of an open project — fast,
AI-built, and now learning to stay clean enough to keep going. The benchmark kept
the *speed* honest; the open question is what keeps the *code* honest, and we're
only just starting to answer it.
